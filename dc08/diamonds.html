In this data challenge we explore the qualities of diamonds and their association with price using the ggplot2::diamonds dataset:

  1. First of all, observe that low quality diamonds (with respect to cut, color and clarity) are more expensive.
  2. Using models, find an explanation and solve the apparent paradox.

Recall that:

  • best cut is Ideal, worst is Fair
  • best color is D, worst is J
  • best clarity is IF, worst is I1

2. Using models, find an explanation and solve the apparent paradox.

We think that there is another variable, influencing both the quality and the price of each diamond, and causing this apparent paradox. We suppose that this variable is carat, and we want to show that.

First, we want to observe the carat variable.

# Scatter plot of price against carat
diamonds |>
  ggplot(aes(carat, price)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.2) +
  geom_smooth(se = FALSE) +
  labs(title = "Price against carat scatterplot")

# Strength of the association
cor(diamonds$carat, diamonds$price, method = "pearson")
## [1] 0.9215913
cor(diamonds$carat, diamonds$price, method = "spearman")
## [1] 0.9628828

We see a strong positive association (Pearson ca. 0.92). The relationship is non-linear: price increases more than proportionally with weight, with a larger spread for heavier diamonds. We can also notice concentrations (vertical lines) around round carat values (0.5, 1, 1.5, 2), likely reflecting buyers’ psychological preferences.

We observe that the medians of carat follow the same trend of those of price, in all the quality variables. This shows that lower quality diamonds tend to be heavier, and suggests that their higher weight raises their price more than their lower quality reduces it. We verify this hypothesis with a model.

We want to fit a model between carat and price, so let’s try a linear one.

The residuals of the linear model still show a clear pattern with respect to carat: the relationship between carat and price is not linear, so the model does not fully capture the effect of weight.

To fix this, we use the same model, but we transform the variables using the logarithm.

diamonds2 <- diamonds2 |>
  mutate(log_price = log(price), log_carat = log(carat))

# Scatter plot on the log scale
ggplot(diamonds2, aes(log_carat, log_price)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.1)

# Linear model with log scale
mod_log <- lm(log_price ~ log_carat, data = diamonds2)
summary(mod_log)

The relationship becomes linear on the log scale. We therefore fit the same linear model on log(carat) and log(price). The residuals of this model show no pattern with respect to carat: they represent the part of the price that is not explained by weight.

Once the effect of weight is removed, the relationship between quality and price is the expected one: for the same carat, better cut, color and clarity correspond to higher prices. The apparent paradox is explained by carat: low quality diamonds tend to be heavier, and weight is the most important factor in determining price.
