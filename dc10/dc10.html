We notice that we have some aggregates for the countries, such as World, Africa, Asia, …

We should get rid of them, otherwise we will consider the data many times for each country. We also notice that Kosovo does not have an ISO code, so we should not discard it with the other aggregates.

Let’s build two data frames, one keeping only the countries with a valid ISO code, plus Kosovo, which does not have an official ISO code, the other with the total global data. We will only keep the variables we need for our analysis.

Two key observations emerge from the historical trend of global CO2 emissions:

  • Following each decline, emissions have resumed their upward trajectory relatively quickly.
  • Historically, major reductions in global CO2 emissions have coincided primarily with economic crises and wars, rather than climate policies or deliberate environmental action.

# Total emissions per 25-year period, with all years up to 1900 in one group
last_year  <- max(co2_world$year)
period_len <- 25
first_year <- min(co2_world$year)
cutoff     <- 1900

periods <- co2_world |>
  mutate(end = last_year - (last_year - year) %/% period_len * period_len,
         period = if_else(year <= cutoff,
                          paste0(first_year, "-", cutoff),
                          paste0(end - period_len + 1, "-", end))) |>
  group_by(period) |>
  summarise(emissions = sum(co2)) |>
  mutate(share = emissions / sum(emissions))

Global emissions are highly concentrated: in both rankings, the ten largest emitters account for roughly 69% of the total. The two rankings, however, tell different stories. China is by far the largest emitter today, with almost a third of global annual emissions, but the United States remains the largest historical emitter, having released about 50% more CO₂ than China since 1750. Several early industrialised countries, such as the United Kingdom, France, Canada and Ukraine, belong to the historical top ten but not to the current one; the United Kingdom, the birthplace of the industrial revolution, ranks fifth historically yet accounts for less than 1% of today’s emissions. Conversely, recently industrialised economies and oil producers such as Iran, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia and South Korea appear only in the annual ranking. Since CO₂ persists in the atmosphere for centuries, warming depends on cumulative rather than annual emissions. This makes the question of historical responsibility central to climate negotiations.

For most of the twentieth century the United States was by far the largest emitter, peaking around 2005 and declining since, while early industrialised European countries have been slowly decreasing since the 1970s. After 2000 China’s emissions grew explosively, reaching more than twice those of the United States, and India is now the third largest emitter and still rising, and they both are on a growing trajectory.

Per capita emissions tell a different story from total emissions. The highest values are found in small oil and gas-producing countries such as Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain and the United Arab Emirates, along with a few small island territories: they have small populations and energy-intensive economies, so they weigh little on the global total. Among the largest historical emitters, the United States, Canada and Russia emit around three times the global average of 4.7 tonnes per person. China emits less than half as much per person as the United States, despite being by far the largest emitter in absolute terms, and India stays well below the global average at about 2.2 tonnes. Per capita emissions therefore add a third dimension to the question of responsibility, alongside current and historical emissions.

Coal was the dominant source of CO₂ emissions until the late 1960s, when oil overtook it with the post-war expansion of road transport and industry. Since the early 2000s coal has regained first place, driven mainly by China’s rapid industrialisation, and global emissions from all three fuels are still growing. The fuel mix varies widely between countries: coal accounts for about three quarters of fossil emissions in China and about 70% in India, while it has almost disappeared in the United Kingdom, Canada and France, where emissions come mostly from oil and gas. The countries whose emissions have already peaked are largely those that moved away from coal, while those still growing depend heavily on it.
